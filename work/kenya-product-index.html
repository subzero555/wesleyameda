Kenya Product Index

A national index of Kenyan brands, ranked by a score no brand can buy.

The problem

Ask which bank in Kenya actually treats people well, or which insurer pays out, and there is nothing to look up. What exists is a Facebook thread, a friend with an opinion, and a page of search results written by the companies being asked about. Everyone is deciding on anecdote.

The review sites that do exist mostly average stars, which fails in two directions at once. A brand with four ratings sits level with a brand that has four hundred, and the loudest customer counts for as much as the most careful one. Then the ranking quietly becomes something a brand can pay to improve, and the whole thing stops being worth reading.

A ranking a brand can buy is not a ranking. It is an advertisement with a number on it.

What it does

People review and rate the Kenyan brands they actually use. Those signals aggregate into a public score and a rank position for every brand inside every sector, recalculated as opinion moves. Each brand gets a page that ranks in search and that the brand itself cannot edit.

  • The KPI Score, a weighted model rather than an average of stars
  • Rankings by sector, from banking and telecom to aviation and healthcare
  • Polls with anonymous voting, so an opinion costs nothing to give
  • An editorial layer of written analysis, with its own admin and scheduling
  • An embeddable badge a brand can put on its own site
  • Accounts, email verification, moderation queues and three staff roles
  • A published methodology, because a score nobody can interrogate is a rumour

The score is the product

The KPI Score is a weighted average multiplied by a credibility factor. Every rating carries the weight of the contributor behind it, and a recent opinion counts for more than one from two years ago. The credibility factor then discounts thin evidence, so a score resting on three opinions does not get to look as solid as one resting on a hundred.

Rank position falls out of the score, then out of the volume of evidence behind it, then out of the name so the order does not drift between runs. A brand with no reviews and no votes gets no position at all rather than being sorted to the bottom. It has not been judged, so there is nothing honest to say about where it stands.

The formula I inherited added half a point for a verified badge, three tenths for featured placement and two tenths for sponsorship, which meant a brand could buy a full point of score. I took all three out. Paid placement can decide where a brand is displayed. It must never decide what a brand scores, and the calculation now takes no commercial input at all, so there is nothing left to quietly turn back on.

The badge that refuses to flatter

A brand can paste one snippet and show its live score on its own homepage. It is given away rather than sold, because every badge is a link back from a company's own domain, and charging for it would only mean fewer of them.

The part worth pointing at is what it does when a brand has no ratings. It says so. It shows no number, and the API returns null rather than zero, so nobody drawing the badge themselves can render an unrated brand as a bottom score either. A badge sits on the one page a brand fully controls, which makes it the one place an invented number would look most official and be questioned least. The setup panel also states plainly that the badge keeps showing the score when it falls, and that there is no setting to freeze it.

How it runs

Next.js on the App Router in TypeScript, PostgreSQL behind Prisma, sessions on signed cookies, and mail through the domain's own SMTP. It is self-hosted on an Ubuntu VPS under PM2 rather than a managed platform, reached through a Cloudflare Tunnel so the firewall never has to open a port inbound at all.

Most pages are built ahead of time and revalidated, because the whole proposition rests on ranking in search for terms like best bank in Kenya. The sitemap is generated from the database per request, after a spell where it was frozen at build time and had quietly stopped listing anything added since the last deploy.

What it taught me

Breadth is not depth. The index went from 160 brands to over a thousand in a single pass, and the honest result was a queue of nine hundred entries a person still has to approve one at a time. Pages with nothing on them do not merely fail to help. They pull down the pages that would have worked.

The other lesson is that a check which cannot fail is not a check. The audit proving the badge escapes untrusted text ends by rendering the same payload unescaped on purpose, and the run fails if that control does not trip. Several things in this codebase reported success for weeks while doing nothing, and the ones I trust now are the ones I have watched break.

Where it goes

Reviews are the binding constraint and no amount of engineering removes it. Everything the platform eventually sells, the brand dashboards, the sentiment tracking against a competitor, the market intelligence, depends on there being enough opinion in the index to be worth reading. The build order follows that rather than the other way round.

What a brand will pay for is not a better position. It is the ability to reply in public, to invite its own customers without screening for the happy ones, and to watch what is being said about it. The product is the independence, and it is the one feature that stops working the moment it is for sale.
